# Creative direction

Make videos that feel like a smart person showing you something worth knowing. They should be enjoyable to watch, useful afterward, and visually composed with care.

## Starting visual language

- Aim for a polished editorial explainer: real details and environments, paired with crisp custom graphics, diagrams and purposeful animation.
- For designed graphics, the local starter pack offers deep navy (`#0A2544`), near-white (`#F5F9FF`), vivid blue (`#2D53F2`, `#43A8FF`) and cyan (`#51DCFE`). Treat these as a starting palette, not an identity requirement; follow any approved brand pack selected for the project.
- Keep filmed people and places natural and warm. Use a restrained accent color only when it clarifies hierarchy or highlights the key idea.
- Use clean sans-serif typography, generous spacing, large readable labels and short on-screen copy. Keep paragraphs in narration; make graphics carry only the words viewers need to understand the image.
- Give each video one subject-specific visual motif. Animate cause and effect, build diagrams as the explanation unfolds, and let important images settle before moving on.
- The local JStudio wordmark is not an approved personal or corporate mark. Leave it out of public work by default; do not invent a personal logo or channel identity.

## Story and voice

- Open on a vivid question, surprising result, useful promise or in-progress action. Skip greetings and throat-clearing.
- Teach one main idea per video. Give it a concrete example, demonstration or comparison that makes the reasoning visible.
- Build a small arc: curiosity → context → discovery or explanation → takeaway. The ending should resolve the opening and leave the viewer with one useful next thought or action.
- Write for speech. Use short, natural sentences, specific nouns and active verbs. Keep technical terms only when they earn their place, and explain them in context.
- Use humor, personality and surprise where they grow naturally from the subject. Never trade accuracy or clarity for a punchline.
- Support factual claims with trustworthy sources. Identify uncertainty; do not invent numbers, testimonials, scenes or results.

## Picture and motion

- Give every shot a job: reveal, demonstrate, compare, orient, emphasize or let an idea land.
- Prefer visual evidence: the real object, process, screen, place or person; a clean diagram; a purposeful metaphor; or animation that shows cause and effect.
- Use varied scale and framing to guide attention. Keep compositions intentional, uncluttered and legible on a phone.
- Use motion to explain relationships or carry attention to the next beat. Keep stillness when it gives the viewer time to read or think.
- Choose a small, coherent color and type system for the specific film. Strong contrast, consistent alignment, restrained accents and careful spacing create polish.
- Avoid ornamental title-card sequences, random stock montages, noisy transitions and effects added only to make the video feel busy.

## Sound and captions

- Make speech clear and present. Use music to shape feeling and pace, mixed below speech; use sound effects only when they clarify or add a deliberate beat.
- Caption all spoken words. Use accurate timing, clean line breaks, comfortable reading speed, high contrast, and placement clear of platform controls.
- Confirm that the first few seconds work with the sound off, while the full story remains satisfying with sound on.

## Review questions

1. Would the intended viewer choose to keep watching after the opening?
2. Can they explain the one central idea afterward?
3. Do the visuals show or clarify what the words claim?
4. Does the pacing include both momentum and time to understand?
5. Does the finish feel intentional in picture, captions and sound?

If an answer is no, revise the story or the specific beat that causes it before adding polish.
